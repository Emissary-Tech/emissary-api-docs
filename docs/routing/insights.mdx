---
title: 'What emerges from routing'
description: 'Routing does more than pick a model. It produces audit-level intelligence about your workload, and it sets up dedicated inference and post-training, so routing compounds over time.'
---

The obvious reasons to route are cost, performance, and latency. The less obvious ones emerge from
routing itself. A router classifies every request against every model and keeps the result. That
gives you two things a single-model setup can't:

1. **Intelligence about your workload**: what's easy, what's hard, and what you're missing.
2. **A path to your own models**: dedicated inference for the open models you route to, and
   post-training on the trajectories your traffic leaves behind.

## What you learn

### What's recorded for every request

For each request, the router records:

- **Each model's confidence** that it could complete the request.
- **The routing reason**: why a particular model was chosen, including whether the request fell to
  the default because nothing was confident, or escalated because the only confident model was more
  expensive.

You see both by clicking into a request in **Activity**. The detail also shows an estimated cost
per candidate, the saving against the baseline, and an execution trace. See
[monitor and debug](./guides/monitor-and-debug).

### Which models you're missing

The router predicts confidence across all models, including ones you haven't enabled, and lists
them all under **Classifier probabilities** in each request's detail. If a model
outside your catalog is confident on a large share of your traffic, that's a candidate to add,
whether for cost, speed, or quality. See [migrate to new models](./guides/migrate-to-new-models).

### Which requests nothing can handle

Requests that end in `default_no_confident` are the ones **no model in your catalog could vouch
for**. Looking at what they have in common tells you where your capability gaps are:

- If they're a coherent type of task, that's a candidate for a **stronger model**, or for a model
  of your own, trained on that task. See
  [from routing to your own models](#from-routing-to-your-own-models).
- If they're scattered, your threshold may be set too high, and the router is being cautious on
  requests that a lower threshold would handle.

### Where your default model is doing the work

A high share of traffic on the default model tells you the catalog's cheaper models aren't
covering enough, or that the default is carrying work a cheaper model could do. Frequent
**escalation** says the opposite: the default is too weak for the traffic it receives.

### How difficult your traffic really is

Spread across models, with confidence, shows what share of your workload is easy and what share
needs a frontier model. Teams often guess at this, and the routing record measures it.

## From routing to your own models

Routing is the first step of a loop: **route, serve, post-train.** Each step makes the next one
possible, and the last makes routing cheaper again.

```
        ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
        ▼                                                           │
     route ──▶ serve open models ──▶ post-train on trajectories ────┘
   (decide)    (dedicated capacity)   (your model joins the catalog)
```

### Dedicated inference

Routing sends a lot of work to open models, and **shared endpoints price those models for the
average workload, not yours.** Dedicated inference runs them on compute that's tuned to your
traffic shape, so the cheap option stays cheap and predictable:

- **Dedicated capacity**, with no noisy neighbors.
- **Tuned to your traffic shape**, rather than to an average.
- **Predictable latency under load.**

The router shows you which models carry your traffic, which tells you what to put on dedicated
capacity. See [inference](../inference/concept) and [deployments](../inference/deployments).

### Post-training

Every routed request leaves a **trajectory**. Post-training turns those trajectories into a model of
your own, specialized to the work you actually do:

- **Cheaper** on work you've already seen.
- **Better on your domain** than any general model.
- **Faster**, because it's smaller.

When a model you trained becomes the better call for a request, the router sends it there
automatically. It's just another model in the catalog, scored for confidence like the rest. See
[post-training concepts](../fine-tuning/concept).

Your data trains your models, and your signal never trains anyone else's.

:::note
Training uses the trajectories your traffic leaves behind, so it needs that data to be kept. With
**zero data retention** on, which is the default, Emissary doesn't retain input, output, or session
replay data. See [monitor and debug](./guides/monitor-and-debug#sessions-and-replay).
:::

### The loop

**Routing feeds your training data, and training makes routing cheaper.** The more you route, the
more trajectories you have to learn from. The routing record shows which requests nothing handles
confidently, which are the best candidates for a model of your own. And the router decides, request
by request, when that model is the better call.

## Why it's useful

Most model decisions are made on public benchmarks and intuition. Routing data comes from *your*
requests, so it answers questions specific to your workload: which model should I add, where is my
catalog weak, what should I serve on dedicated capacity, and what should I train next.
